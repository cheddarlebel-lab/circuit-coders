Executive Summary

This audit was not commissioned. We ran it because a national sweep of low-rated franchise dealerships across 54 metros surfaced this store second from the bottom, and the reason turned out to be more interesting than the ranking.

Of the 3,368 low-rated dealerships we measured nationwide, only one carries a worse BBB rating score than this rooftop. Yours is 14.0 out of 100. We expected to find a badly run store. The evidence says otherwise.

Your store is not badly rated. It is unevenly rated, and it is also unevenly named. On Google, the dealership at 16835 Katy Freeway holds 4.5 stars on 4,540 reviews — a large, healthy base that took years to build. On Yelp, the same address holds 2.4 stars on 150 reviews, claimed but unworked, with 82 further reviews suppressed by Yelp's filter. BBB grades the same address F.

The second finding is one we have not seen this clearly anywhere else in the sweep, and it is free to fix: this dealership is publishing itself under three different names, across two separate Google listings, at two adjacent street numbers. Section 4 lays it out. It is very likely costing you search visibility you have already paid for.

One thing sits outside the reputation frame and we would rather say it than sell around it. A published BBB customer review alleges a specific F&I sequence — an advertised price, a different lot price, and a $1,998 option package described as mandatory that an employee allegedly admitted was removable. Independent complainants describe a warranty product added without authorisation. Review management does not fix that. Section 3 covers it plainly, because a vendor who sold you review-generation and stayed quiet about it would be taking your money under false pretenses.

The pattern is not random.

The platform at 4.5 is the one your CRM solicits automatically after a sale. The platform at 2.4 only receives reviews people take the trouble to leave unprompted — which, without a pipeline pointed at it, means almost exclusively the aggrieved. A 2.1-star spread is not a difference of opinion among customers. It is the difference between a platform somebody owns and a platform nobody has logged into.

2The BBB F — Second Lowest in a 3,368-Dealer Sample

Hard defect
A BBB rating score of 14.0 out of 100, with a Pattern of Complaints alert attached.

BBB's two stated reasons, verbatim: "Failure to respond to 5 complaint(s) filed against business" and "4 complaint(s) filed against business that were not resolved." Not accredited. Fifteen years in business.

Of the ten most recent complaints BBB publishes, five are marked Unanswered and two more closed Unresolved. The unanswered ones run from June 2025 through May 2026.

BBB's algorithm punishes silence far more heavily than it punishes the underlying complaint — a business that answers every complaint and resolves none still outranks one that ignores five. Five unanswered disputes is not a quality failure. It is an inbox nobody owns.

The Pattern of Complaints alert is the part that compounds.

BBB applies that flag when it judges a business has failed to resolve the underlying cause of repeated complaints, rather than any single incident. It is the difference between a grade you can answer your way out of and one that needs a process change. Answering the backlog will move the letter; it will not lift the alert on its own.

3What the Complaints Actually Say

We read the published texts rather than just the counts, because the counts alone would have led us to sell you the wrong thing.

Stated plainly before anything else in this section.

Everything below is an allegation made by a member of the public on a platform that publishes complaints without adjudicating them. Circuit Coders takes no position on whether any of it is accurate, and we are not lawyers. We are flagging it because these are the words currently sitting on a page that ranks for your store's name.

Theme one — the service department

Five of the ten most recent complaints are service or repair issues, and they are the serious kind: a 2026 vehicle allegedly damaged while in the dealership's care during a warranty transmission repair; an engine failure on a 2022 AT4 with 70,000 miles; a 2023 Yukon Denali described as service negligence. Long, detailed, and unanswered.

Theme two — title and registration

A customer who purchased in late March 2025 states that as of August 1 they had still not received registration or plates, having been told to keep waiting. Whatever the cause, a four-month tag delay is a documentary matter with a paper trail, and it is the kind of complaint that escalates to a state regulator rather than staying on BBB.

Theme three — F&I products added or described as mandatory

This is the theme we would look at first, because it appears in both a complaint and an independent published review:

  • A June 2025 complainant alleges "misrepresentation of vehicle features and unauthorized addition of warranty product."
  • A BBB customer review describes a vehicle advertised online at $15,622 with a lot tag of $15,964, then a finance office figure inflated to roughly $18,000 before tax by a $1,998 option package. The reviewer states an employee "slipped up and admitted the package could be removed" while a manager "lied saying it was mandatory."

We are not adjudicating either account. But the shape is a disclosure-timing problem: a qualifying charge exists somewhere, and buyers are meeting it in the finance office rather than in the advertisement. Moving one disclosure line from the footer into the offer is close to free, and it retires an entire complaint category.

Theme four — a promotion that did not pay out

A complainant documents a "Test drive your Envision today, receive a $100 reward card" offer, completed with an emailed and texted appointment confirmation, and states the card never arrived. This one is marked Resolved — which is worth noting, because it proves the store can close a complaint properly when it engages. That is the argument for doing it on the other nine.

Name, address and phone consistency is the single input Google weighs most heavily when deciding whether two records describe the same real business. Right now yours disagree with each other on the name in three different ways, and a second listing two street numbers away is carrying eight reviews at 3.0 under a name that reads like the parent company.

Two consequences, both concrete:

  • Review dilution. Every review that lands on the 8-review listing is a review that did not land on the 4,540-review one. At that ratio the second listing contributes nothing and costs you a low-rated result on your own brand name.
  • Customers cannot name you. In the BBB complaints themselves, the same store is called "Classic Elite Buick GMC," "Classic Buick GMC," and "Classic Elite GMC" by three different people. One complainant opened by clarifying which dealership they even meant. When customers cannot reliably name the business, neither can a search engine.

Consolidating or clearly differentiating the second listing, then pushing one canonical name and address everywhere, is a week of work and requires no vendor's permission.

5What Is Genuinely Well Run

Credit where it is earned — and the reason this is worth doing.

A store with real problems on the floor does not hold 4.5 stars across 4,540 reviews for fifteen years. The operation is working.

  • 4,540 Google reviews at 4.5 is a top-tier base for a single Buick/GMC rooftop, and it is the raw material any recovery runs on.
  • The review pipeline clearly works. Nothing produces 4,540 reviews by accident — somebody built that process. The entire proposal in this document is "point the thing you already built at the platforms it is not pointed at."
  • Reviewers name the service department positively on the Google listing, which matters given that service is where the complaints cluster. Both things are true at once; the unhappy ones are simply the only ones with a megaphone on Yelp and BBB.
  • One complaint was carried to Resolved, with the complainant confirming satisfaction. The capability exists. It has been used once out of ten.
  • Fifteen years in business under a recognised Houston name.

7The Program

Everything below is work we do, not advice we hand you.

Recovery sprint — the first 30 days

  • Answer the BBB backlog. All five unanswered complaints drafted, reviewed by you, filed. Where a complaint is resolvable we pursue the resolution, not just the reply — you have already proved that works once.
  • Resolve the listing split. Consolidate or clearly differentiate the 16833 'Classic Elite Auto Family' listing, then push one canonical name, address and phone across Google, Yelp, BBB, KBB, Cars.com, DealerRater, CARFAX, Apple and Bing.
  • Open Yelp. Response templates for the existing 150, then every new review answered inside 48 hours. Public, non-defensive, signed.
  • Attack the suppressed pile. 82 filtered reviews are not lost — Yelp's filter re-weights as legitimate accounts gain activity, and the fix is volume from real buyers, not argument.
  • Escalate the title-and-registration complaint out of the queue. A four-month tag delay is a documentary matter; it goes to you directly in week one with the internal question written out.
  • Disclosure review on the option-package and warranty-product themes in Section 3. We mark up the current advertising and F&I language and hand you a revised version. You decide whether to adopt it.

Ongoing, monthly

  • Every review on every platform answered within 48 hours, in your voice, signed by a real person.
  • A review pipeline pointed at the unmanaged platforms — the fix for Yelp is not arguing with the reviews you have, it is giving the next several hundred satisfied buyers somewhere to go.
  • BBB complaint monitoring with a named owner, so the grade never regenerates from silence.
  • Listing integrity monitoring — new duplicates, drifting hours, changed numbers.
  • Monthly written report: what moved, what did not, what we recommend next. Plain English.

11Method

So you can check every number rather than trust it.

  • Discovery. BBB's public search API, filtered to F-rated new- and used-car dealers across 54 U.S. metros. 3,368 low-rated dealerships returned; 1,128 franchise rooftops after removing national chains and manufacturer corporate records.
  • BBB. Public profile and complaints pages, read in full. Rating reasons, complaint status counts, business details and contacts quoted verbatim.
  • Google. Business Profile and Maps listing read directly, including the review summary panel, owner-response behaviour and the local competitive set.
  • Yelp. Live listing, first page of reviews read in full, owner-response state and suppressed-review count taken from the page itself.
  • Identity check. Both Google listings, the Yelp listing, the BBB record and the Houston Automobile Dealers Association directory entry were compared on name, street number and phone to confirm they describe one business.
  • No access, no scraping of private systems, no contact with your staff or customers. Everything here was readable by anyone with a browser.
